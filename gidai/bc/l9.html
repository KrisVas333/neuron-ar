Lesson 9

“Neuron is stronger when he’s not alone.”

🎯 WHAT THIS LESSON WANTS FROM YOU: 

teach one word — GLIA (the brain’s helper cells; our Glia is a star cell) · 

one technique — THE HOMERIC NAME (name + a true, kind tag: “swift-footed Achilles”), said with a smile · 

one belief — “A smile and a name are enough to make a friend.” If the kids leave with those three, you won.


“After today you’ll be able to make friends just by smiling and remembering names.”  (the Promise — Slide 6, checked on Slide 18)


WHAT THE KID WALKS OUT WITH: A Homeric name for their partner (and one for themselves), the proof that their own name cuts through noise, and a Friendship Paper that levelled up.

WHAT THE PARENT HEARS AT HOME: “Why did you call me that? What does my name mean? I’ve got a Homeric name for you.”

WHAT YOU WALK OUT KNOWING: Your own name is so important to your brain that about a third of people hear it in a voice they are deliberately ignoring (the “cocktail party effect”: Moray 1959; Wood & Cowan 1995, 34.6%).

✅ SUCCESS: within 1–2 weeks a kid greets someone new by name with a smile, or tells you the Homeric name they gave a parent  ·  ❌ FAIL: on Slide 18 the kids can’t say why they heard their name in the noise or how Homer made his heroes memorable.

⏱ LATE? THE 60-SECOND PREP:– Noise Card printed, names of kids present written in, a steady reader picked.– Every headset: charged, Flying Squirrel Chase installed, same L5 pairs, each pair in its own private room with voice on. Casting ready.– Smile + Name boxes cut, L5 Friendship Papers + glue on the table. Video (Slide 12) loaded, sound checked.

★ NAME IN THE NOISE (SLIDES 9–11): TWO VOICES AT THE SAME TIME. NEVER CUT IT.

1 · Before class: print the Noise Card, write a few names of kids who are present into the blanks.
2 · Slide 9: pick one volunteer reader. Tell the class: listen to MY story, 2 questions after, ignore the noise. Don’t mention the names.
3 · Slide 10: on your nod you BOTH start: you read the Iliad passage, the volunteer reads the Noise Card out loud at the same time. Kids listen to you.
4 · Slide 11: the 2 questions about the story, THEN “Hands up if you heard YOUR name.”

SLIDE 7“Where were YOU when I lost?”3 min (9–12) · 60′: 2 (7–9)
  • Show the slide, say nothing for 3 seconds. “The shadow was her. She smiles at him.” Click, read Neuron grumpy: “Where were YOU when I lost?” ❓ “Is that a nice way to say hello?”
  • Click: Glia holds out the oxygen orb. Softly: “I was fetching you oxygen.” Pause. ❓ “So where was she, really, while he was losing?” (Working for him, out of sight.)
  • Her smile does the talking: “A smile is a language. Everyone in the world speaks it.”
  • ⚠ Say once: “Real glia don’t talk or have feelings. Our Glia is a story character. But glia are real. Want to know what they do?”
SLIDE 8In your real brain — glial cells = helper cells3 min (12–15) · 60′: 2 (9–11)
  • “Your brain has neurons, the messenger cells, and glial cells, the helper cells. People thought glia were just glue; the name even means ‘glue’ in Greek. Wrong: they’re a whole crew of helpers, about one for every neuron.”
  • ⚠ Say it clearly: “Our Glia is an astrocyte — a STAR cell. Wrapper cells and patrol cells are other kinds of glia.” (The ⭐ card says “= OUR GLIA”.)
  • Click ⭐ Star cells (astrocytes): sit between tiny blood vessels and neurons; when neurons get busy, they help send more blood there, and blood carries oxygen. “That’s what Glia was fetching.”
  • Click 🧥 Wrapper cells (oligodendrocytes): wrap the neurons’ long cables in a “speed jacket” called myelin. Messages zip faster.
  • Click 🔦 Patrol cells (microglia): check everything around them, all the time, and clean up mess. ❓ “Which job would you want?” (2 answers; 60′: skip the question.)
  • ⚠ Don’t say: “glia talk to neurons” · “glia wash your brain at night” · “screens damage glia” · “myelin is armour” · “10 glia per neuron” · “exercise grows glia”.
  • 🔬 About 86 billion neurons and about as many other cells, roughly 1:1, not the old “10 glia per neuron” myth (Azevedo et al. 2009, J Comp Neurol; von Bartheld et al. 2016, J Comp Neurol). Astrocytes help match blood flow to neuron activity (Attwell et al. 2010, Nature — ⚠ verify). Oligodendrocytes make myelin (Nave 2010, Nature); in young mice, learning a skill drives new myelin (McKenzie et al. 2014, Science). Microglia constantly survey tissue (Nimmerjahn et al. 2005, Science).
SLIDE 9TEST · Name in the Noise — the setup2 min (15–17) · 60′: 1 (11–12)
  • ⚠ Before class: the printed Noise Card has the names of a few kids present written in the blanks (never the reader’s own name; empty blanks crossed out).
  • Pick a volunteer who reads steadily. Give them the card. They stand at the back or side: normal volume, flat voice, about one word a second, names in the same flat voice.
  • “I’ll read you a short story. Listen hard: 2 questions after. At the same time, [name] reads random words. Your job: ignore the noise.” Don’t mention the names.
SLIDE 10LISTEN. — The Iliad, Book 62 min (17–19) · 60′: 2 (12–14)
  • ⚠ Both voices at once: you read the passage below; AT THE SAME TIME the volunteer reads the Noise Card. Start together on your nod. The kids listen to you.
  • One breath of setup: “This is from the Iliad, a story Homer told 3,000 years ago. Hector is the great hero of the city of Troy. He’s just come back from the fighting.” Nod, start. Read slowly, about 70 s:
  • “Hector came back from the battle, still in his armour. At the gate his wife Andromache ran to meet him, and with her came the nurse, carrying their baby son, a little child, lovely as a star. Hector smiled as he looked at the boy, but he said nothing. Then he stretched out his arms to hold his son. But the baby cried and hid his face in the nurse’s arms. He was scared of his father’s shining bronze helmet, and of the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely on top. His father and mother laughed to see it. So Hector took the helmet off his head and laid it, all gleaming, on the ground. Then he took his darling child, kissed him, and rocked him in his arms.”
  • When you finish, stop the reader. If the reader runs out of words first, they go back to the top.
  • 🔬 Iliad Book 6 (Hector and Andromache at the Scaean Gate), a shortened retelling of Samuel Butler’s 1898 prose translation (public domain).
SLIDE 112 questions + the reveal4 min (19–23) · 60′: 3 (14–17)
  • ❓ 1. What scared the baby? → his dad’s helmet with the nodding horse-hair plume. ❓ 2. What did Hector do with his helmet? → took it off and put it on the ground. Hands up for each.
  • Click: “Hands up if you heard your own name, even though you were listening to me.” Count the hands out loud. Ask the reader: ❓ “Which names did you read?”
  • Click: “Your brain was ignoring the noise, and still your name got through. Your name is that strong.” Not everyone hears it; that is part of the finding too.
  • 🔬 The “cocktail party effect”: people listening to one voice often notice their own name in the voice they ignore (Moray 1959, Q J Exp Psychol). In a replication about a third noticed it (34.6%; Wood & Cowan 1995, J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn). 🧰 Predict → reveal · live experiment.
SLIDE 12Video · The sweetest sound (EN, 65 s)3 min (23–26) · 60′: 3 (17–20)
  • Play it, full screen, whole class once: EN video on Drive.
  • Then ask out loud (not on screen): ❓ “Whose name do you remember from today, and how?” 2–3 answers; praise any hook (“the girl who read the noise card”).
  • 🔬 Dale Carnegie, How to Win Friends and Influence People (1936): “a person’s name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language”; also his rule “Smile”. Advice from a book, not a lab finding; the lab finding is Slide 11.
SLIDE 13EXTRA · Warm-up: Mirror + Fast Feet6 min (26–32) · 60′: skip (90 min only)
  • ⚠ 90-min classes only (LT, Ireland). Chicago (60 min): skip this slide.
  • L5 partners (absent partner: make a trio), standing face to face, an arm’s length apart.
  • MIRROR (3 min): “One leads, one is the mirror. Copy every move and every face, slowly.” Swap after 20 s, 3–4 rounds. Faces count: smiles, surprise, grumpy Neuron.
  • FAST FEET (3 min): Rock-Paper-Scissors with feet. Jump on the spot, “rock, paper, scissors, GO!”, land: rock = feet together · paper = feet wide · scissors = one foot forward, one back. Best of 3. No winners announced. Can’t jump? Seated, with hands.
  • 🧰 Body first. Source: PABUDIMAS no-video list, D3 Mirror and D1 RPS Fast Feet (Active Schools).
SLIDE 14TECHNIQUE · Homeric names7 min (32–39) · 60′: 4 (20–24)
  • “Remember Hector and the baby? That was Homer. 3,000 years ago he told the Iliad out loud. Nobody could read. So he gave every hero a name plus a little tag: swift-footed Achilles. Hear it once, and you remember who’s who.”
  • Read out 4–5 real ones, then ❓ “Which one would you want?”
    swift-footed Achilles Hector of the shining helmet Hector, breaker of horses white-armed Andromache rosy-fingered Dawn grey-eyed Athena wise Odysseus (“of many plans”) Agamemnon, lord of men Nestor, the clear-voiced speaker of Pylos
  • Nothing is written on screen: the Vibers invent every one. Character by character: ❓ “Neuron the… what?” 2–3 suggestions each, the room picks one by cheering. True and kind (Brain Rot excepted). Stuck? Back-pocket: “Brain Rot the Sloppy”, “Glia the Resourceful”.
  • 🔬 Homer’s epithets helped singers and listeners keep track of heroes in an oral performance (Milman Parry’s oral-formulaic studies, 1928–30 — ⚠ verify). “Hector, breaker of horses” is the Iliad’s very last line. 🧰 Generation (kids make the tags) · dual coding (name + picture).
SLIDE 15Now your partner — Name + the ______5 min (39–44) · 60′: 3 (24–27)
  • “Now give your partner a Homeric name. Three rules: it’s TRUE, it’s KIND, and they say YES.” Stuck? Ask first: ❓ “What’s something you’re good at? What do you love?”
  • Click: “If they say no, that’s fine. Pick another.” Then each kid says it back with a smile: “Hi, Tom the Tireless.”
  • ⚠ Walk round. Stop anything that’s a joke at someone’s expense. A kid can opt out and keep just their name.
SLIDE 16VR · Flying Squirrel Chase + the 3 name questions25 min (44–69): 3 headsets on + 22 play · 60′: 23 (27–50): 3 + 20 play · never under 20 min of play
  • ⚠ CASTING FIRST then headsets: casting button in the casting Viber’s headset (red) → web casting → oculus.com/casting/brainclubpbcinc618757 → code → go. Straps: back first, top last. Out of VR: Meta button = home · double-tap = passthrough · lift it off.
  • Game: Flying Squirrel Chase, same game and same partner as L5 (it must pass the 4 gates before the lesson). “In L5 we showed genuine interest in our partner. Now we do it again, and this time we talk about NAMES while we play.” The 3 questions stay on screen.
  • ⚠ The metronome: about every 7 min (60′: every 6) call the next question loud: “Question 1! What nicknames have you ever had?” · “Question 2! What does your name mean?” · “Question 3! What would you name your son or daughter?”
  • One asks, the partner answers, swap. After each answer: “And then what?” Use your partner’s Homeric name when you ask. Don’t know what your name means? Say so: it’s homework.
  • Headsets on: 3 min (casting first, straps). Play: 22 min (60′: 20), never under 20 min of play. Headsets off → straight to Slide 17. The names get written down on the Friendship Paper (Slide 19, 90 min only).
  • 🧰 Retrieval practice · genuine interest (L5 callback).
SLIDE 17What did we do to our brain??3 min (69–72) · 60′: 2 (50–52)
  • Skills first — ask: ❓ “What did we DO today?” → a) smiled at each other and felt good about it · b) stuck a name + epithet onto a face · c) chased in VR, fast feet, kept our timing.
  • Then regions, a → ORBITOFRONTAL CORTEX (gold), just behind the eyes: the treat detector, a smile feels like a prize · b → HIPPOCAMPUS (light blue): the glue gun · c → CEREBELLUM (pink): the timing coach. This is where a lot of the work happened, not this lives only here.
  • 🔬 Seeing a smiling face raises activity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex, a reward area (O’Doherty et al. 2003, Neuropsychologia — ⚠ verify). The hippocampus helps bind new face-name pairs (Sperling et al. 2003, NeuroImage — ⚠ verify). The cerebellum fine-tunes the timing and accuracy of movement (textbook).
SLIDE 18🏆 What did we learn today?5 min (72–77) · 60′: 3 (52–55)
  • Four questions, kids answer first:
  • ❓ 1. Where was Glia when Neuron lost? → Fetching him oxygen. Her kind (star cells) helps send blood and oxygen to busy neurons.
  • ❓ 2. Why did some of us hear our name in the noise? → Your name is so important to your brain that it gets through even when you’re ignoring a voice (the cocktail party effect).
  • ❓ 3. How did Homer make us remember his heroes? → A name plus an epithet: swift-footed Achilles.
  • ❓ 4. What was today’s promise? → Make friends just by smiling and remembering names.
  • 🧰 Active recall · say the goal / check the goal.
SLIDE 19FRIENDSHIP PAPER · LEVEL UP!7 min (77–84) · 60′: skip (90 min only)
  • ⚠ 90-min classes only (LT, Ireland). Chicago (60 min): skip this slide.
  • Hand out the L5 Friendship Papers (lost ones: fresh copy) and the printed SMILE + NAME box. “Remember: this paper may increase in value. Today it does.” Glue the box on.
  • By hand, all three boxes: partner’s name + Homeric name · one thing you learned about them · Homeric names I can remember (from memory, no peeking). Count how many — just for you, no ranking.
  • Check 2 papers.
  • 🔬 Pulling a name back out after a gap beats re-reading it (retrieval practice; Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Psychological Science — ⚠ verify). 🧰 Retrieval after a gap.
