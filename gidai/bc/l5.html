Lesson 5

“The Alien Test.”
▶ OPEN THE SLIDES — L5 V1 EN BC

AT A GLANCE — THE 1-MINUTE VERSION

🎯 WHAT THIS LESSON WANTS FROM YOU: 

teach one word — genuinely — the difference between asking and actually wanting to know · 

one technique — the One-Question Challenge — ask one question you don’t know the answer to, listen, be able to repeat the answer · 

one belief — “People like me more when I’m interested in them than when I’m interesting.” If the kids leave with those three, you won.


“By the end of today you’ll know the ONE thing that makes aliens — and humans — want to know you.”  (the Promise — said at Slide 4, on screen at Slide 10)


WHAT THE KID WALKS OUT WITH: The notebook — the L5 artefact, in their hands from Slide 5 — with page 1 (who the aliens should meet, and why) and page 2 (the adult at home + the question they’ve never asked). And one real thing they learned about a classmate they didn’t choose.

★ WHAT THE PARENT HEARS AT HOME — IMPORTANT: “Can I ask you something I’ve never asked you?” — that’s the homework, and it’s the sentence a parent notices. Say it in the room word for word (Slide 17 and Slide 19) — this is the one line a parent repeats to other parents.

✅ SUCCESS: within 1–2 weeks a kid asks someone a real question and can repeat the answer — a parent, a sibling, a classmate they didn’t pick  ·  ❌ FAIL: kids can’t say why the aliens pick Neuron, not AItor (Slide 8), or nobody in the room is surprised at Slide 12 or Slide 15 — that means nobody actually asked.

⏱ LATE? THE 60-SECOND PREP:– Notebooks + pens on every seat before Slide 5 — they come out with the aliens, not at Slide 17. Never announce the answer: if you say “Neuron is right”, the lesson is a lecture.– Pair list written BEFORE class (Slide 9) · casting + one Puzzling Places room per pair BEFORE Slide 14, codes on the board.– Landing (Slide 1) is 60–90 s of quiet BEFORE the Oath. Do it, don’t describe it.

★ CASTING — SET IT UP AT SLIDE 13, BEFORE ANY HEADSET GOES ON. OR THE INTERVIEW PUZZLE NEVER STARTS.

1. Casting button in the casting Viber’s headset (the red one) → 2. Web casting → 3. Classroom computer: oculus.com/casting/brainclubpbcinc618757 → 4. Enter the code → 5. Let it rip. Then: Puzzling Places → multiplayer → one private room per pair (host = one kid per pair; the casting headset hosts its own pair) → write the room codes on the board → guests join. In-game voice OFF.

4 → 5⚡ Activation game — HUMAN RADAR2 min
  • ⚠️ Claude-drafted on Kris’s ask (“insert a physical activation game/exercise 1–2 min”) — swap for any game you like, but keep its one rule: you can only find out by ASKING.
  • Everyone up, chairs pushed in. “Three rounds, twenty seconds each. Round one: find someone in this room who has the same number of brothers and sisters as you. Hand on their shoulder when you’ve found them. There’s only one way to find out — ask. GO.”
  • Round two: “…born in the same season as you.” Round three: “…hates the same food as you.” Count the last five seconds down out loud each round.
  • Nobody found one? Fine — the asking was the game, not the finding. Sit. Same seats for now — the new partner comes at Slide 9.

SLIDE 6AItor vs Neuron — who should the aliens meet?2 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: A and B are characters from our story. ⚠️ Claude’s pick: A = AItor, B = Neuron. Deck slide 6 still says PERSON A / PERSON B — the two columns below are the proposed slide copy.
  • A = AItor. Knows a LOT — billions of facts about Earth · talks about what he knows · never asks a question (“ion care”) · answers before you’ve finished the sentence.
  • B = Neuron. Asks questions · remembers what you said ten minutes ago · genuinely surprised by your answers · says almost nothing about himself — he’s one of 86 billion.

SLIDE 7Write it → the mentor reads it out → open talk → verdict5 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: the A-vs-B debate is scrapped — no teams, no 20-second turns. Deck slide 7 still says LEFT half / RIGHT half — read past it until it’s changed.
  • 2 min silent writing — notebook, page 1. “Who should the aliens meet — AItor or Neuron? Write your pick and three reasons. Nobody talks yet.” Writing first is the point — arguments that exist before the noise are stronger than arguments made up in it.
  • 2 min: collect the notebooks, read 4–5 aloud. No names. Alternate an AItor pick with a Neuron pick. Reading them yourself is the point — a shy kid’s reason gets the same air as a loud kid’s.
  • 1 min open talk + verdict. ❓ “Who changed their mind hearing someone else’s reason?” Then: “The whole class has to agree on ONE. Go.” They will land on Neuron. If they land on AItor anyway: ❓ “Would you want to sit next to AItor for an hour?”
  • Keep the register on “what’s attractive?”, never “what’s nice”. This is not a manners lesson. Hand the notebooks back at Slide 9, when they change seats.
SLIDE 8The aliens’ verdict — the principle2 min
  • “The aliens pick Neuron. Every time. And a man called Dale Carnegie wrote down why — ninety years ago. It’s still rule number one for making friends.”
  • Read the line on the slide once, slowly: “Become genuinely interested in other people.”
  • “Not ‘be interesting’. Be interested. AItor was a walking Wikipedia — and nobody wanted to meet him.”
  • ❓ “What’s the difference between interesting and interested?” Listening for: interesting = about me · interested = about you.
  • The extra takeaway (Kris, 2026-09-15): “AItor knows a billion things. Neuron knows one — and he asked about YOU. Robots are smart. People are interesting. Every single person in this room is. That’s why the aliens picked a person.” ⚠️ wording Claude-drafted from Kris’s note — adjust to your voice.
SLIDE 9Why it works — the brain fact + micro-move3 min
  • Read the slide. Then the plain version: “When you ask a real question, you hand the other person’s brain a small reward. That’s the trick. And it only works if it’s real — brains are very good at spotting fake interest.”
  • Source if a kid asks: Tamir & Mitchell, 2012 — people in a scanner even gave up small amounts of money to talk about themselves. Reward regions lit up. Don’t oversell it; it’s one study, it’s solid, that’s enough.
  • Micro-move, everyone up: “Ten cross-crawls — right elbow to left knee, swap. Go.” Then the pairs — assigned by YOU before the lesson, never chosen in the room. “Now, partners for the rest of today. [read the pairs]. And no, you don’t get to pick. The aliens aren’t watching how you talk to your best friend.” Hand the notebooks back as they move. Slides 12, 14 and 15 all run on this pair.
  • The pair list, built before class: each Viber with the kid they’ve spoken to least (judge from L1–L4 seating) · never repeat a pair from an earlier lesson — keep the list, it’s reused all season · cross the friend clusters · quiet veto for a known conflict, no public reason · odd number → the spare Viber partners you, and you answer the questions too. Never say “you were paired because you don’t know each other” — that shames both kids.

SLIDE 12The One-Question Challenge — the rehearsal for the headsets4 min
  • Read the four steps. 1 min each way — call the swap out loud. Then 1–2 min of “what surprised you” to the whole group. This is the rehearsal — in the headsets they do the same thing five times, with a follow-up question.
  • Surprise is the proof. If nobody is surprised, nobody asked — the questions were too safe. Say: “Ask something you’d actually want to know. ‘What’s your favourite colour’ is not a question, it’s a form.”
  • Listening for, at the share-out: a fact the partner didn’t already know, said with a small “huh”. That “huh” is the lesson landing.

SLIDE 14Co-op VR — INTERVIEW PUZZLE (Puzzling Places, multiplayer)1 + 11 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: the game is Puzzling Places — two players, one private room per pair, same puzzle. Interview Golf / Walkabout is gone. Deck slide 14 still says INTERVIEW GOLF; the new slide copy is in Claude outputs/BC_L5_VR_swap_PuzzlingPlaces_PLAN.md § 4. Untested — 2-headset test before Sept 28 (plan § 7).
  • FRAME IT (1 min) — “Your brain is training INTEREST today, not speed. Two of you, one puzzle, one room. Your hands will be busy — which is exactly why this works: it’s easier to say a true thing when you’re not staring at someone’s face. I’ll call out a question every few minutes. You both answer it. Then the only rule that matters: after they answer, you ask ONE more question about what they just said. The second question is the real one. And when the headsets come off, you introduce your partner to this room. So listen like you’ll have to repeat it — because you will.”
  • PLAY (11 min) — pairs build; you call the questions out loud to the whole room, one every ~2.5 min (at 0 · 2.5 · 5 · 7.5 · 10). Both partners answer every call, then the follow-up. Watch the cast, listen to the room: who asks a second question, who answers in one word, who goes silent and just builds. Note two names for Slide 15.
  • The five calls (rotate the alternates so a pair never gets the same set twice):
    1 · “What’s something you’re weirdly good at that nobody in this room knows?”
    2 · “What’s something you used to love doing and stopped? What happened?”
    3 · “Who’s the easiest person in your life to talk to — and what do they do that makes it easy?”
    4 · “What’s something you want to try but haven’t, because it feels a bit scary?”
    5 · “What’s the nicest thing anyone has said to you that you still remember?”
    Alternates: the best thing that happened last week · the first ten minutes after you get home · what people usually get wrong about you · if school closed for a month, what would you actually learn · what you changed your mind about this year · the one thing you own you’d keep · what you want this room to know you for by the end of the year.
  • One “pass” per kid is allowed and it’s not a failure — but they still have to ask. If an answer goes somewhere heavy (loss, home, being left out): thank them, move to the next call, follow up privately after the lesson. Never probe in the room.
  • Never past 25 minutes in VR. At 11 minutes of play, call it — the puzzle isn’t the point. Early finishers: headsets off, keep going through the questions face to face.
  • If the rooms fail on the day: Open Brush, pairs build ONE object together, each holds half the brief — same question calls, same follow-up rule.
SLIDE 15Headsets off — INTRODUCE YOUR PARTNER6 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: no minute of silence — headsets off, straight into the round. Deck slide 15 still says “one minute of silence” and shows the four golf questions — the round below replaces them (plan § 5).
  • Round the room, standing (4 min): “This is [name]. The thing that surprised me was ___.” One sentence, 15–20 seconds each. The partner gives a thumbs up — or corrects exactly one thing. One correction max, then move on.
  • Nothing about the puzzle. If all a kid has is how the build went, they say that honestly — that’s information, not a failure. Lots of thumbs up = they listened. Lots of corrections = they heard and didn’t listen — a better lesson than any slide.
  • Two questions, not four (2 min): ❓ “When was it easiest to work together? When was it hardest?” Two or three voices each, no more. Over 12 kids → visible timer, one sentence each, no exceptions.
SLIDE 16What did we do to our brain? + Block 1 — call and response, not a speech4 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: “not the mentor delivering a 5-min monologue”. Every line below is a question first, the answer second. Skills tied to the exact moment, then the region — “this is where a lot of the work happened”, not “this lives only here”.
  • ❓ “Hands up if you asked a second question in the puzzle.” → a) asking instead of telling — Prefrontal Cortex, the boss saying “not yet” to your own mouth.
  • ❓ “Hands up if something your partner said surprised you.” → b) listening for the surprise — Attention network, pointed at someone else on purpose.
  • ❓ “Hands up if it felt good to be asked.” → c) being asked — Reward system, the same one that fires for food and music. That feeling was real.
  • Block 1 in five words — the kids read them off the slide, you point: Viber · SPOT · Amygdala · AItor · interested. ❓ “Which one did you use this week without noticing?” Two voices, no more.
  • Capability, as a fact: “You can now ask a question about another person and actually listen to the answer.” Then the healths in one line, no more: “Today was mental health — connection. And information health — what they said versus what you assumed.”
  • Close with the promise: ❓ “Did we deliver? What’s the ONE thing that makes aliens want to know you?” Listening for: being interested in them. Cut for time: the “whose Lesson 1 goal was about friends” turn — use it only if a kid’s goal genuinely was.
