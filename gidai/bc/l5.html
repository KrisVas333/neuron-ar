🎯 WHAT THIS LESSON WANTS FROM YOU: 

teach one word — genuinely — the difference between asking and actually wanting to know · 

one technique — the One-Question Challenge — ask one question you don’t know the answer to, listen, be able to repeat the answer · 

one belief — “People like me more when I’m interested in them than when I’m interesting.” If the kids leave with those three, you won.    REWRITE THE belief. it seems like the goal is to make the kids more likeable, whereas the goal is to help them make friends. it is a slight but important difference. maybe "the way to make friends is to be interested in other people" or something.

✎ NEW belief: “The way to make a friend is to be genuinely interested in them.” — about finding friends, not about being liked.

WHAT THE KID WALKS OUT WITH: The notebook — the L5 artefact, in their hands from Slide 5 — with page 1 (who the aliens should meet, and why), page 2 (the Memory Map — five places on a planet, each named after a classmate’s memory) and page 3 (the adult at home + the question they’ve never asked). And five real stories from a classmate they didn’t choose.

★ WHAT THE PARENT HEARS AT HOME — IMPORTANT: “Can I ask you something I’ve never asked you?” — that’s the homework, and it’s the sentence a parent notices. Say it in the room word for word (Slide 17 and Slide 19) — this is the one line a parent repeats to other parents.

SLIDE 9Why it works — the brain fact + micro-move3 min
  • Read the slide. Then the plain version: “When you ask a real question, you hand the other person’s brain a small reward. That’s the trick. And it only works if it’s real — brains are very good at spotting fake interest.”
  • Source if a kid asks: Tamir & Mitchell, 2012 — people in a scanner even gave up small amounts of money to talk about themselves. Reward regions lit up. Don’t oversell it; it’s one study, it’s solid, that’s enough.
  • Micro-move, everyone up: “Ten cross-crawls — right elbow to left knee, swap. Go.” Then the pairs — assigned by YOU before the lesson, never chosen in the room. “Now, partners for the rest of today. [read the pairs]. And no, you don’t get to pick. The aliens aren’t watching how you talk to your best friend.” Hand the notebooks back as they move. Slides 12, 14 and 15 all run on this pair.
  • The pair list, built before class: each Viber with the kid they’ve spoken to least (judge from L1–L4 seating) · never repeat a pair from an earlier lesson — keep the list, it’s reused all season · cross the friend clusters · quiet veto for a known conflict, no public reason · odd number → the spare Viber partners you, and you answer the questions too. Never say “you were paired because you don’t know each other” — that shames both kids.

SLIDE 12The One-Question Challenge — the rehearsal for the headsets4 min
  • Read the four steps. 1 min each way — call the swap out loud. Then 1–2 min of “what surprised you” to the whole group. This is the rehearsal — in the headsets they do it five more times — and each answer becomes the name of a place.
  • Surprise is the proof. If nobody is surprised, nobody asked — the questions were too safe. Say: “Ask something you’d actually want to know. ‘What’s your favourite colour’ is not a question, it’s a form.”
  • Listening for, at the share-out: a fact the partner didn’t already know, said with a small “huh”. That “huh” is the lesson landing.

SLIDE 13Before the headsets — both rules, casting, lobbies2 min
  • Both games are explained HERE, before any headset goes on — never inside VR. Read them exactly: “Game one, TWO EYES: one of you is the Scout — you fly high and you may only DESCRIBE. The other is the Keeper — you must FIND three things the Scout describes. No ‘over there’, no pointing — words only. We swap at halfway. Game two, MEMORY MAP: I’ll call out a memory every two minutes. One of you tells the story. Then you find a place on your planet that fits it — and you name it after the story. A name only counts when you BOTH say it out loud.”
  • 15 seconds of finger-to-thumb taps, both hands. Second micro-move of the lesson — do it with them.
  • Casting BEFORE anyone puts a headset on — see the red box at the top. Then one private lobby per pair, one planet per pair, written on the board. This is the difference between a room that launches and ten stuck kids.
  • Straps: back straps first, then top strap. Escape hatches, said once: Meta button = home · double-tap = passthrough · lift off. Flying makes some kids dizzy — anyone who feels sick lifts the headset and becomes the Scout’s voice from outside. That’s a role, not a failure.
  • Pairs stay the pairs from Slide 9 — not best friends. In each pair, Viber A = the one sitting on the left. You’ll need that for the memory calls.
SLIDE 14Co-op VR — TWO EYES → MEMORY MAP (Flying Squirrel Chase)6 + 1 + 10 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-23: the game is Flying Squirrel Chase (free, PEGI 3, multiplayer, already on the fleet) — a one-off for L5. Puzzling Places is on hold; Interview Golf is gone. Still untested with two headsets — private lobby, per-pair voice, one planet per pair. If the lobbies fail on the day, see the fallback at the bottom.
  • Why two games: free flight has no turns, and turns are where questions live. So the game carries the pair — and you are the metronome. Nothing personal happens unless you call it.
  • TWO EYES (6 min: 3 + 3) — Scout describes, Keeper finds three things. At 3:00 call “SWAP!” over the room. This warms every voice up with zero social risk — they’re describing a planet, not themselves. Listen for “over there” and bounce it: “Words only. What does it LOOK like?”
  • “SWITCH — MEMORY MAP!” (1 min) — both land somewhere together. “Now the planet has no names. You give it five. Every name is a story.”
  • MEMORY MAP (10 min) — call one memory every ~2 minutes, out loud, to the whole room. Calls 1 · 3 · 5 = Viber A tells the story, calls 2 · 4 = Viber B. The partner listens, then they find a place that fits and both say the name.
    1 · “Viber A: tell your partner about the best day you remember from last summer.”
    2 · “Viber B: a time you got in trouble — and it was worth it.”
    3 · “Viber A: the weirdest thing you’ve ever eaten, and where you were.”
    4 · “Viber B: something that happened to you that nobody believes.”
    5 · “Viber A: the funniest thing that ever happened in your family.”
    Reserves (fast pairs, or a call that lands badly): a time you were really scared, and what happened after · a place you’ve been that you still think about · a time you were really good at something. Rule: a memory, never an opinion — “tell me about the time…”, not “what’s your favourite…”. Memories give stories; opinions give one word.
  • Your lines while they fly: a pair goes silent → “You’re allowed to be nosy. Being nosy politely is the whole skill.” · a generic name (“Big Rock”) → “That’s a rock’s name. Give it your partner’s name for it.” · watch the cast: who tells, who only flies, who names everything alone. Note two names for Slide 15.
  • A kid may pass on one memory — the partner picks a reserve call instead. If a story goes somewhere heavy (loss, home, being left out): thank them, move to the next call, follow up privately after the lesson. Never probe in the room.
  • Never past 25 minutes in VR. At 17 minutes of play, call it — mid-flight if needed. LT 90 min: TWO EYES 8 (4 + 4), MEMORY MAP 12 (six calls, three each).
  • If the lobbies aren’t private, or all pairs land in one voice channel: Open Brush co-op build — pairs build one object, each holds half the brief; same five memory calls, and each part of the object gets named after one.
SLIDE 15Headsets off — the map goes in the notebook, then INTRODUCE YOUR PARTNER4 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: no minute of silence — headsets off, straight into the notebook.
  • Notebook, page 2 (1 min): “Write your five names — and next to each one, whose memory it is.” Pairs may whisper to check. This page is a map of a person, not a planet.
  • Round the room, standing (3 min): “This is [name]. This place is called ___ — because ___.” One place, one sentence, 15–20 seconds each. The partner gives a thumbs up — or corrects exactly one thing. One correction max, then move on.

SLIDE 16What did we do to our brain? + Block 1 — call and response, not a speech4 min
  • Kris, 2026-09-15: “not the mentor delivering a 5-min monologue”. Every line below is a question first, the answer second. Skills tied to the exact moment, then the region — “this is where a lot of the work happened”, not “this lives only here”.
  • ❓ “Hands up if you can still tell one of your partner’s stories.” → a) asking instead of telling — Prefrontal Cortex, the boss saying “not yet” to your own mouth.
  • ❓ “Hands up if something your partner said surprised you.” → b) listening for the surprise — Attention network, pointed at someone else on purpose.
  • ❓ “Hands up if it felt good to be asked.” → c) being asked — Reward system, the same one that fires for food and music. That feeling was real.
  • Block 1 in five words — the kids read them off the slide, you point: Viber · SPOT · Amygdala · AItor · interested. ❓ “Which one did you use this week without noticing?” Two voices, no more.
  • Capability, as a fact: “You can now ask a question about another person and actually listen to the answer.” Then the healths in one line, no more: “Today was mental health — connection. And information health — what they said versus what you assumed.”
  • Close with the promise: ❓ “Did we deliver? What’s the ONE thing that makes aliens want to know you?” Listening for: being interested in them. Cut for time: the “whose Lesson 1 goal was about friends” turn — use it only if a kid’s goal genuinely was.
