BrAIn Club

BrAIn Club – an after-school programme where kids aged 9–14 train their brains the way athletes train their bodies – building focus, emotional skills and healthy tech habits through cognitive science, VR missions and a story where the child is the hero.
In one word, it's a gym for the brain

What happens in a lesson (90 minutes)

This is the answer when a parent asks "so what do they actually do there?" — each beat comes with a real example you can quote.
~15 minRitual + discussion
~15 minWarm-up activity
20–30 minVR mission
~15 minDebrief + consolidation
  • Ritual + discussionWhat it is: kids learn the club's rules and its identity mantra — the Viber Oath — and repeat it every time they gather. This opens every lesson, which always starts with an open discussion about the day's topic.
  • Warm-up activityA hands-on activity that teaches the day's skill through the body first — so the VR mission has meaning. Real examples: a follow-along hand warm-up · breathing exercises · a silent voting game where kids answer only with movement — shaking their hands, shaking their head, or stomping their feet. 
  • VR missionThe headset is a training tool, not a prize: each experience is chosen for the specific brain skill it exercises. Real examples: controlling VR from zero (First Steps) · 3D drawing/building in Open Brush with the task "draw one thing that scares you and one thing that calms you down" · building from deliberately confusing AI instructions — then working out why the result came out weird.
  • Debrief + recallHeadsets come off and kids name what their brain just practised — this is what turns play into learning that sticks. Even more important — discussion consolidates the information given during the lesson. 
    Results of creative tasks are presented by the kids themselves in front of the class — encouraging both sharing experiences and public speaking.
    Real example: "You weren't just playing — you were training your brain. Judging distance in 3D space? That's the parietal lobe. Every grab that got more precise? Motor cortex." Then a fast quiz: "How do you exit VR? What do the breathing exercises train?"
  • Reward + closeKids unlock a collectible character, hear a teaser for next time, and one volunteer leads a short closing round — giving feedback on the lesson. Real example: unlocking the character Princess Amygdala, whom kids can meet in augmented reality (AR) through their own mobile devices, outside the club too.

What kids learn — and the parent FAQ

Lessons rotate through four types. Each comes with something a parent can actually see at home:

Physical

Move

Body control, coordination, and the "brake" — the ability to consciously stop. At home: your child shows you the hand warm-up, or explains that moving is "Brain Rot's weakness."

Cognitive

Train

Attention, memory, focus — and the ability to spot what's stealing them. At home: your child points at a fast-cut video and names it: "quick cuts, bright colours — that's designed to hook your attention."

Emotional

Regulate

Naming feelings, calming the body with breath, a pause before reacting. At home: your child does a breathing exercise before homework, or tells you about "the almond-sized part of the brain that controls feelings."

Social + AI literacy

Connect & question

Teamwork, presenting to the group — and treating AI as a tool you direct, not an oracle you blindly trust. At home: your child double-checks an AI answer and says: "AI doesn't know anything — it's just the world's best guesser."

By the end of the programme, we want every child to be able to say three things:
I found my best friendBelonging and real social connection — group builds, presentations, a different child leading the closing round each week.
I got to know myselfUnderstanding their own attention, feelings and habits — down to naming the brain regions they trained that day.
I built a healthier relationship with technologyKids are in charge of technology, not the other way round — they set their own boundaries in VR and learn to question AI rather than blindly trust it.
All three add up to one outcome we tell parents: a child ready for the future.

Frequently asked by parents

ASKIs VR safe for my child? What about their eyes?
SAY

Headsets are used only under mentor supervision, and VR time is capped at 20–30 minutes of a 90-minute lesson — everything else happens off-screen. Before playing anything, every child learns three ways to exit the headset — that's the very first VR skill we teach, in lesson one. Mentors watch how kids are doing and pause immediately if needed.

ASKIsn't this just more screen time? I'm trying to cut down.
SAY

We agree with you — that's exactly what this programme is for. Kids examine why they want to spend time on screens and learn how to resist it. For example, we discuss why endless short videos hook them so hard — quick cuts, randomness, over-bright colours — and connect that to how they feel afterwards: passive and unmotivated. Then we train the opposite: creating instead of consuming, moving instead of scrolling, and setting their own boundaries. The VR portion is short, purposeful and curated. 

ASKMy child wears glasses / gets motion sick. Can they join?
SAY

Most glasses fit under the VR headset, and mentors help every child adjust the fit and clarity. On motion sensitivity: the experiences we use are gentle and mostly static — drawing, building, guided programmes — sessions are short, and a child can step out at any time. If your child has a specific health concern, we'll connect you with the programme team.

ASKIs it just games? What does my child actually get out of it?
SAY

Every VR experience is chosen for the skill it trains, and after every session kids take the headset off and name what their brain just practised and which region did the work. A concrete example: in the AI lesson, kids build from deliberately confusing instructions, see the result come out weird, and work out for themselves that a confusing task gives a confusing result — that's critical thinking about AI, learned through play. This is a brain-training programme that uses games — not a gaming club.

ASKWho are these characters my child keeps talking about?
SAY

The programme runs on a story set in BrAIn City. Your child's guide is Neuron ("there are 86 billion of us in your head"). The villain is Brain Rot: he embodies what endless scrolling does to attention and motivation. Princess Amygdala rules the feelings, and AItor is the AI — "the world's best guesser," who always needs double-checking. After every lesson kids unlock a character carrying a real science fact they can teach you at home. If your child tells you a brain fact at dinner — the programme is working.

ASKDoes my child need any equipment or experience?
SAY

No — we provide everything, including the headsets, and no VR, coding or other prior experience is needed. Lesson one starts with putting on a headset for the very first time. Equipment is cleaned between uses and set up by the mentor before every lesson.
