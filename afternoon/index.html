A manifesto

The
Afternoon

AI is giving kids their mornings back. Almost nobody is talking about what happens next.

The academic morning is being compressed. Not someday — now. What used to take five hours of a child sitting still can increasingly be done in two, with a tutor that never gets tired and never gets impatient with the kid who reads slowly.

I was that kid. I couldn't read a page until I was eleven. If that tutor had existed, my entire childhood would have been a different shape.

So the morning gets shorter. Good.

The question nobody is answering is what fills the space.

Because the space is real, and it is large.

180afternoons a year, per child

That is not "free time." It is the largest unallocated block of a childhood — and right now it gets allocated by default. By whatever is easiest. By whatever is already open on the phone.

About 11,000 kids a year come through our robotics clubs. So I can tell you what happens in an afternoon that has been claimed on purpose, and it isn't what parents expect.

The robot is not the point. The robot is bait.

What actually happens is that a nine-year-old discovers a thing which didn't work can be made to work — by her, twice, on purpose. That's the whole product. Everything else is packaging.

And an afternoon that gets surrendered? Nothing dramatic happens. That's the trap. No child is destroyed by a phone. They are just not there. Consuming instead of creating. It costs nothing, and it costs everything.

So here is what I'm building toward — and I intend to be tedious about it for the rest of the year.

AI is giving kids their afternoons back. My job is making sure they use them to become extraordinary.

Not busier. Not more scheduled. Not optimised.

Extraordinary — which mostly means having made something, having been bad at it first, and having had a person nearby who expected them to finish.

Mornings are becoming academics.
Afternoons are where a person gets made.

That's the thesis. I'll spend the next decade on it.

And if you're a parent booking September right now — you are not booking a class. You are allocating 180 afternoons. Choose like it.

Kris Vasiliauskas is a Lithuanian education founder. Dyslexic, couldn't read until eleven, went on to build one of the country's largest robotics academies — about 11,000 children a year — and now works across Lithuania, Ireland and Chicago on what children do after school.
